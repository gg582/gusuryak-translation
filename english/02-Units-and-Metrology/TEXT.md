## 數名苐二 — The Names of Numbers: Chapter 2

名者數之紀也所以稽實而辨數者也
A name is the guiding thread of numbers; it is the means by which actual quantities are reckoned and numbers distinguished.

有物有期有名矣故數名次之十而止
Things have fixed limits, hence they have names, and the names of numbers divide their sequence by tens.

故數名次之數一十百千萬也
Therefore the names of the numbers forming the sequence are one (一), ten (十), hundred (百), thousand (千), and ten thousand (萬).

所以算事物順性命之理也
This is for reckoning the quantities of things and for following the principle of nature and destiny (性命).

傳曰先其算命本起於黃鍾之數
A commentary says, "First calculate their number and name." The root begins with the number of the Yellow Bell (黃鍾).

一而三之三三積之歷十二辰之數
十有七萬七千一百四十七
Starting from 1, triple it, and keep multiplying by 3 again and again until reaching the number corresponding to the twelve Earthly Branches (十二辰): it becomes 177,147.

紀於一協於十長於百大於千衍於萬而五數備矣
Taking its thread from one, harmonizing at ten, growing at a hundred, becoming great at a thousand, and unfolding at ten thousand — thus the five numbers are complete.

夫推歷生律制器 規圜矩方 權重衡平 準繩嘉量 探賾索隱 鉤深致遠 莫不用焉
Indeed, in projecting the calendar and generating the pitch pipes, and in making vessels; in using the compass to draw circles and the carpenter's square to form rectangles; in fixing weights with the counterpoise and balancing with the beam; in using the water-level and the ink-line and finely adjusted standard measures; in probing the abstruse and seeking out the hidden, in drawing up the deep and reaching the far — there is nothing in which arithmetic is not used.

度長短者不失毫釐 量多少者不失圭撮 權輕重者不失黍累
In measuring long and short, they err not by so much as a hao (毫) or li (釐); in measuring much and little, not by a gui (圭) or cuo (撮); in weighing light and heavy, not by a millet grain (黍) or lei (累).

度起於忽蠺吐之絲 忽為毫 十忽為絲 十絲為毫 十毫為釐 十釐為分 十分為寸 十寸為尺 十尺為丈 十丈為引 五尺為步 或六尺
The units of length begin with the hol (忽). A single filament of silk spat out by a silkworm is called a hol; ten hol are gathered to make one sa (絲), and ten sa are gathered to make one ho (毫). Ten ho make 1 ri (釐), 10 ri make 1 bun (分), 10 bun make 1 chon (寸), 10 chon make 1 chi (尺), 10 chi make 1 jang (丈), and 10 jang make 1 in (引). Five chi are taken as 1 bo (步), though in some cases six chi are taken as 1 bo.

八尺曰尋 倍尋曰常端法五丈或四丈 匹法三丈二或二丈四東俗匹法四丈或三丈五 中國畒法

Eight chi are called a sim (尋), and twice a sim is called a sang (常). The rule for the dan (端) is 5 jang or 4 jang, and the rule for the pil (匹) is 3 jang 2 chi or 2 jang 4 chi. Our country's customary pil rule (東俗匹法) is 4 jang or 3 jang 5 chi, and

中國𤱈法二百四十歩里法三百六十歩或三百歩
the Chinese mu (畝) rule takes 240 bo as 1 mu, while the li (里) rule takes 360 bo or 300 bo as 1 li.

東俗田一萬尺為結百尺為負十尺為束一尺為把田尺見大典
In our country's custom of land measurement, 10,000 square chi are taken as 1 gyeol (結), 100 square chi as 1 bu (負), 10 square chi as 1 sok (束), and 1 square chi as 1 pa (把); the field chi (田尺) appears in the "Gyeongguk Daejeon" (大典).

量起於圭六粒之粟十圭為撮十撮為抄十抄為勺十勺為合
Volume (量) begins with the gyu (圭), which is the amount of six grains of foxtail millet (粟). Ten gyu make 1 chwal (撮), 10 chwal make 1 cho (鈔), 10 cho make 1 jak (勺), and 10 jak make 1 hap (合).

黄鍾之實千二百黍實其龠両龠為合十合為升十升為斗十斗為斛又謂之石
The actual capacity of the Yellow Bell pipe is such that 1,200 grains of millet (黍) fill its one yak (龠); two yak make 1 hap (合), 10 hap make 1 seung (升), 10 seung make 1 du (斗), and 10 du make 1 gok (斛), which is also called a seok (石).

古有豆區釜鍾庾秉今比日不用東俗以十五斗為石
In antiquity there were the du (豆), gu (區), bu (釜), jong (鍾), yu (庾), and byeong (秉), but these are no longer used nowadays; in our country's custom, 15 du are taken as 1 seok.

𢖍起於黍一粒之粟
The balance (權衡) begins with a millet grain (黍) the size of one grain of foxtail millet (粟).

千黍為絫
One thousand millet grains make 1 ryu (絫),

十絫為銖
and ten ryu make 1 ju (銖),

八銖為錙
while eight ju make 1 ji (錙).

六銖為分
Six ju make 1 bun (分),

四分為両
and four bun make 1 ryang (兩).

黄鍾之實千二百黍
The substance of the Yellow Bell (黃鍾) pipe is 1,200 millet grains,

其重十二銖
and its weight is 12 ju.

両之為両
Doubling this makes 1 ryang.

十六両為斤
Sixteen ryang make 1 geun (斤),

即三百八十四銖
that is, 384 ju.

三十斤為鈞
Thirty geun make 1 gyun (鈞),

四鈞為石
and four gyun make 1 seok (石).

後世両下有錢分整里毫不用銖絫
In later ages, below the ryang (兩) there are the jeon (錢), bun (分), ri (釐), and ho (毫), so the ju (銖) and ryu (絫) are not used.

十五斤為一穪
Fifteen geun are called 1 ching (稱),

二百斤為引
and 200 geun are called 1 in (引).

錢鈔千文為母貟
One thousand mun (文) of paper money and copper coins gathered together are called 1 won (圓),

五貫為錠
and five gwan (貫) are called 1 jeong (錠).

東俗一百斤為一稱
In the custom of the Eastern Country (Joseon), 100 geun are called 1 ching,

小錢四百為両
400 mun of small coin are called 1 ryang,

大鎬一百為両
and 100 mun of large ho coin are called 1 ryang;

千文為錢
1,000 mun are called 1 jeon (錢).

錢鈔附見于此
The units of paper money and copper coins are appended here.

度以尺為君有匹則匹為君
For length (度), the chi (尺) is taken as the sovereign (君), but when there is a pil (匹), the pil is taken as the sovereign;

量以石為君無石則斗為君
for volume (量), the seok (石) is taken as the sovereign, but when there is no seok, the du (斗) is taken as the sovereign;

權𢖍以両為君有斤則斤為君
and for weight (權衡), the ryang (兩) is taken as the sovereign, but when there is a geun (斤), the geun is taken as the sovereign.

君煮成數之主
The sovereign becomes the master of the complete number (成數).

一千百千萬是謂正數
One, ten, hundred, thousand, and ten thousand are called the regular numbers (正數),

億延京域科穰溝澗正載是謂大數
and eok (億), ja (延), gyeong (京), hae (域), seo (科), yang (穰), gu (溝), gan (澗), jeong (正), and jae (載) are called the great numbers (大數),

分啓厘毫絲忽㣲纎沙塵竢側漠是謂小數
while bun (分), rim (厘), ho (毫), sa (絲), hol (忽), mi (微), seom (纖), sa (沙), jin (塵), ae (埃), byeong (渺), and mak (漠) are called the small numbers (小數).

大數之上推之至於無極
Extending upward from the great numbers, one reaches the limitless (無極),

小數之下推之入於無倫
and extending downward from the small numbers, one enters the incomparable (無倫).

此聖人所謂語大莫能載語小莫䏻志者毁
This is what the sage meant when he said, "Speaking of the great, nothing can contain it; speaking of the small, nothing can record it."

## Commentary

### Powers of 3

3 to the 0th power is ja (子).
3 to the 1st power is chuk (丑).
3 to the 2nd power is in (寅).
3 to the 3rd power is myo (卯).
3 to the 4th power is jin (辰).
3 to the 5th power is sa (巳).
3 to the 6th power is o (午).
3 to the 7th power is mi (未).
3 to the 8th power is sin (申).
3 to the 9th power is yu (酉).
3 to the 10th power is sul (戌).
3 to the 11th power is hae (亥).

Here, each position of the twelve jin (辰) is matched to consecutive powers of 3 starting from 1, and the final value is 3¹¹ = 177,147.

### The Powers of Ten

This is discussed again in the Gusuryak's Suwi Je-sam (數位第三, Chapter 3: The Place-Value Expression of Numbers).

The units place is     一.
The tens place is      十.
The hundreds place is  百.
The thousands place is 千.
The ten-thousands place is 萬.
*From here on, the scale ascends in units of 10 to the fourth power, reusing 1 through 1,000.*
The 100,000,000 place is 億.
The 1,000,000,000,000 place is 兆.
The 10,000,000,000,000,000 place is 京.
The 100,000,000,000,000,000,000 place is 垓.
The 10^24 place is 秭.
Beyond these there are 穰, 溝, 澗, 正, 載, and 極.

Beyond those there are Ganges-sand (恒河沙), asaṃkhya (阿僧祇), nayuta (那由他), the inconceivable (不可思議), and the immeasurable great number (無量大數).

These are sometimes written together with decimal points, but fractions approximating the value are mostly used.

Very small quantities — the decimal places — are as follows.

0.1 is 分.
0.01 is 釐.
0.001 is 毫.
0.0001 is 絲.

After this, decreasing steadily by powers of 10⁻¹, there are 忽, 微, 纖, 沙, 塵, 埃, 渺, 漠, 模糊, 逡巡, 須臾, 瞬息, 彈指, 剎那, 六德, 虛空, and 淸淨.

For example, 3.141592 may, for convenience, be written using the approximation 355/113 = 3.141592920353982.
However, in things like the calendar for knowing the heavens, it is sometimes written in a complicated way, such as 三有一分四厘一毫五絲九忽二微.

By the same principle, names are given in units of 10 for length, grain, and so on, but 5, 6, 8, and others are sometimes mixed in depending on the system of weights and measures.
Typically, even the units hap, doe, mal, and seom used in markets differ by region.
In some regions, 10 hap make 1 doe, 15 doe make 1 mal, and 10 mal make 1 seom; they do not always follow the formula.

This shows that in measuring numbers, the units and their usage matter.

Below I introduce the representative units taught in this book.

First, the chi (尺) of classical metrology differed according to use. Here the cloth chi (pobaek-cheok, 布帛尺) used for measuring cloth is employed, and this corresponds to 1.348 times the Yellow Bell chi (黃鍾尺).

*Eight chi are called a sim (尋), and twice a sim is called a sang (常). The rule for the dan (端) is 5 jang or 4 jang, and the rule for the pil (匹) is 3 jang 2 chi or 2 jang 4 chi. Our country's customary pil rule (東俗匹法) is 4 jang or 3 jang 5 chi, while in China the mu (畝) rule is used.*

Regrettably, modern studies have the limitation of centering on the restoration of documents and the stirring of patriotism.
We are here to learn mathematics, not culture. Following the contemporary records of the Yellow Bell chi, one could also back-calculate taking it as 343.66 mm.

However, in arithmetic it is convenient to use expressions with fractions. Using the time-honored reduced-fraction approximations handed down since the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, let us call it 343 and two-thirds and move on. This is not the exact value, but it is easier to read.

343 ⅔ × 1.348

= 343 ⅔ × 1348/1000

= 1031/3 × 337/250

= (1031 × 337)/(3 × 250)

= 347447/750

= 463 197/750

Because the untidy factor of 1.348 is taken as the basis of the unit, modern conversions will be used for reference only.

If you want a cleaner calculation for an approximate value, you may use the reduced fraction 31/23, which is close to 1.348 and simple in fractional form. Had this system of units survived as a scale compatible with the SI system, perhaps a similar operation would have been performed.

343 ⅔ × 31/23

= 1031/3 × 31/23

= 31961/69

= 463 + 14/69

= 463 14/69

Thus one sim is eight chi, and one sang comes out to sixteen ja (chi).


Now let us work out the ratios between the values.

| Unit          | Ratio |
| ----          | ----  |
| chi:sim       | 1:8   |
| chi:jang      | 1:10  |
| sim:pil (24 chi) | 1:3 |
| sim:pil (30 chi) | 4:15 |
| sim:pil (40 chi) | 1:5  |
| sim:pil (35 chi) | 8:35 |

One can see how complex the system is. First, because the standard of the Yellow Bell chi is fixed by weighing the millet of a particular year, if the standard chi made of bronze wears away and is lost, it cannot be recovered. Second, if sim:pil were made 1:8:24, the ratios would be easy to compute as composites of 1:8 and 1:3, but most were arranged and adjusted with international concerns in mind, so the values are very messy. Third, if the weight of millet changes greatly due to climate change, past administrative records become badly distorted, causing difficulty in calculating national income, economic indices, and the like. When this happens, the disaster of having to excavate a standard chi from hundreds of years ago arises.

This is why the SI system was created and came to be used internationally. The SI system established in France can easily be restored even if the standard prototypes disappear.

### Units of Square Dimension

Taking 10,000 square chi as 1 gyeol (結), 100 square chi as 1 bu (負), 10 square chi as 1 sok (束), and 1 square chi as 1 pa (把) means the units proceed by $10^0$, $10^1$, $10^2$, and $10^4$.

If the base number is 10, the ratios follow 10; if the base number is 2, they follow 2; if 2 and 3 together are the base, they follow the ratio of the composite number formed from them. In this way, a standard must first be established as the model of the unit system so that it is convenient for people to read and write.
