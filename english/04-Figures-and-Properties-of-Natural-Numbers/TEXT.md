## 數象苐四 On the Figures of Numbers: Chapter 4

![Counting-rod notation](../../korean/04-자연수의-상과-그-성질에-대하여/CountingRods.png)

Figures of Numbers (數象) Part 4 (On the Figures of Numbers)

象者數之形也所以布䇿而明數者也有位
A figure (象) is the shape of a number; it is the means by which counting rods (策) are laid out to make numbers clear. When there is a place value (位),

斯有象矣故數象次之
there is then a figure; therefore the Figures of Numbers (數象) are placed next.

*Note: counting-rod notation does not appear in Unicode. Below it has been replaced with Indo-Arabic numerals, but the original notation instructions are conveyed in a separate image.*

布筭口訣見啓蒙
The rhymed formula for laying out counting rods is found in the Introduction to Mathematics (算學啓蒙, Sanhak Gyemong).

一從十𢖍百立千僵
For the ones place, lay the rods vertically (從); for the tens place, lay them horizontally (橫); for the hundreds place, stand them upright (立); for the thousands place, lay them down (僵).

千十相並萬百相望
The thousands place and the tens place share the same form with each other, and the ten-thousands place and the hundreds place correspond to each other in form.

滿六以上五在上方
When the count reaches 6 or more, the rod representing 5 is placed on top.

六不積聚五不單張
For 6, six individual rods are not piled up; for 5, a rod is not spread out alone at the bottom.

言十過身不滿自當
When the count passes 10, the place value is carried up; when 10 is not filled, the number is taken as it stands in that place.

若明此訣可習九章
If you clearly understand this formula, you can study the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art (九章算術).

解曰一者單數也綂徴

It is explained: 1 (一) is a single number (a number of the ones place), represented as a whole;

一至九之數十者十數

it is a number from 1 to 9. 10 (十) is a number of the tens place,

九十之數

a number up to 90,

也統一十至

and is likewise represented as a whole from 10 onward.

遇一則下筭遇二則下二筭三泗五放此六則下 一六而加一於上以當五數七則上一下二八則上一下三九則上一下四即河圖一含五而得六二含五而得七三含五而得八四含五而得九也

When you meet 1, place one rod below; when you meet 2, place two rods below; 3, 4 (泗), and 5 follow the same pattern. For 6, place one rod below and add one rod above to stand for the number 5. For 7, one rod above and two below; for 8, one rod above and three below; for 9, one rod above and four below. This is precisely the same principle as in the River Map (河圖), where 1 embraces 5 to make 6, 2 embraces 5 to make 7, 3 embraces 5 to make 8, and 4 embraces 5 to make 9.

人之手指僂數亦象之也布筭一從一𢖍隂陽錯綜之義也

A person's bending the fingers to count likewise imitates this figure (象). When laying out counting rods, one place is vertical (從 = 縱) and one place is horizontal (𢖍 = 橫): this is the meaning of yin and yang interweaving with one another.

從者宙數也𢖍者宇數也傳曰宇宙在乎手非此之謂歟

The vertical is the number of zhou (宙, time), and the horizontal is the number of yu (宇, space). The old saying "the cosmos (宇宙) lies in one's hand" — is it not precisely of this that it speaks?

𢖍與横同從輿縦同

𢖍 is the same as 橫, and 從 is the same as 縱.



## Commentary

The counting-rod notation is a form in which a base-5 system is embedded within a base-10 system. For example, 6 is written as 5+1, and 7 is written as 2+5. When the place value changes, the rods are rotated. Through this, a reading rule arises: rods in the upright orientation occupy the $10^{2x}$ place, while rods in the inverted (rotated) orientation occupy the $10^{2n+1}$ place. This rotation rule is a great help not only for the mathematics of the past, but also for understanding modern techniques. Even setting aside the mathematico-philosophical declaration that the cosmos can be explained through mathematics, this is perfectly clear.

Suppose one were to notate f(x)/f'(x): the derivative is flipped in both orientation (horizontal and vertical) while dropping one dimension. Consider the case where the degree of the derivative was written down incorrectly by mistake. Suppose you intended to notate $f'(x)=3x^2+2$, the tangent line of $f(x)=x^3+2x+1$, but by mistake placed it in the position of $f(x)=3x^3+2x$. If you remembered even the rotation but erred only in the place value, you would immediately know that the position of the derivative notation is wrong, and you would only need to shift it one space to the right. Unfortunately, in the period when Choi Seok-jeong lived, calculus was only just beginning to develop even in the West, and it was of course out of the question that it had been imported into the East. Yet paradoxically, in this era when calculus has been imported and everyone knows the concept of the derivative, the place-value notation of counting rods happens to confer a great advantage at the verification stage. Accordingly, the supplementary commentary to this textbook likewise proceeds using counting rods whenever the state can be reused or whenever verification is important.

Although this passage speaks of the operating principle of the River Map graph, if you dig into the underlying principle, it is in fact speaking obliquely of the base-5 system. Therefore, from now on, when reading and working through Eastern mathematical books, including the Gusuryak, it is good to be proficient in both the base-5 and base-10 systems.

Although this is a cosmological explanation with nothing in particular to work through by way of commentary, the method of using counting rods must be mastered thoroughly at this stage. In the problems to come, such as equations, complicated calculations will be carried out with counting rods or the abacus. To follow along without difficulty, you must know how to use them well.
If possible, it would also be good to try modern numerical-analysis techniques such as Horner's method, the Newton–Raphson method, and Richardson extrapolation using counting rods. Though it is said that one should not kill a chicken with an ox-cleaver, a person who wields the ox-cleaver well also adapts well to the chicken knife.
