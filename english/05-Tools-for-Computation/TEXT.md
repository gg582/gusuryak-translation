## 數器苐五 On the Instruments of Numbers: Chapter 5

The Instruments of Numbers (數器), Chapter 5 (on the instruments of numbers)

器者數之物也所以備物而立數者也

An instrument (器) is the material embodiment of numbers; it is the means by which things are made complete and numbers are established.

有象斯有器矣故數器次之

Where there are forms (象), there are instruments (器); therefore the instruments of numbers (數器) come next.

古者以竹為筭圜徑一分長六寸二百七十一枚而成六觚為一握

In ancient times, counting rods (筭) were made of bamboo: round in cross-section, with a diameter of 1 pun (分) and a length of 6 chon (寸). Gather 271 of them and they form a hexagonal prism, making one handful (一握).

正筭赤負筭黒

The counting rods for positive numbers (正筭) were made red, and the counting rods for negative numbers (負筭) were made black.

後世以珠代筭左實右法而古制失夫

In later generations, abacus beads (珠) replaced the counting rods: the dividend (實, the number to be divided / the result) was placed on the left and the divisor (法, the dividing number / the standard) on the right — and because of this, the ancient system was lost.

近世竹筭不以圜而以三棱

The bamboo counting rods of recent times are not made round but in the shape of a triangular prism (三棱).

凢數生扵律審扵度嘉扵量鉤扵𢖍

All numbers (數) are born from the pitch pipes (律), refined through length measures (度), harmonized through capacity measures (量), and weighed and compared on the balance (𢖍 = 衡).

律度量𢖍數之器也

The pitch pipes, length measures, capacity measures, and the balance (律度量衡) are the instruments of numbers (器).

𢖍運生䂓䂓圜生矩矩方生䋲䋲直生凖

The balance (𢖍) in motion gives rise to the compass (䂓); the compass draws the circle; the carpenter's square (矩) makes the square; the inked line (䋲) makes the straight; and the level (凖) makes the flat.

䂓矩䋲凖器之則也

The compass (規), the carpenter's square (矩), the inked line (繩), and the level (準) are the rules of the instruments.

易曰形而上謂之道形而下謂之器

The I Ching says: "What is above form is called the Way (道); what is within form is called the instrument (器)."

邵子曰筭法之起不岀乎方圎平直此之謂也

Master Shao (Shao Kangjie, 邵康節) said: "The arising of computational methods (算法) never goes beyond the square (方), the circle (圓), the flat (平), and the straight (直)" — and this passage speaks precisely of that.

## 數法苐六 On the Methods of Numbers: Chapter 6

The Methods of Numbers (數法), Chapter 6 (on the methods of operating with numbers)

法者數之用也 所以通變而成數者也

A method (法) is the application (用) of numbers; it is the means by which one penetrates change and brings numbers to completion.

有器斯有法矣 故數法次之

Where there are instruments (器), there are methods (法); therefore the methods of numbers (數法) come next.

神而明之 存乎其人

To apply these methods with divine subtlety and to understand them clearly depends entirely on the person's ability.

## Commentary

*Following the commentary in Chapter 4, I will let 𢖍 carry the sense of 橫 (horizontal) as well.*

Counting rods of the past were sold in bundles of 271, and they were often sold arranged in the shape of the grid of the six-heights diagram of the Luo Writing (nakseo-yuk-go-do, 洛書六高圖) that will appear later, with the center cell filled in. Counting rods were mainly made triangular, so as to make them easy to put in and take out.

However, modern mathematics involves very complex calculations, and in the course of use one ends up piling more than 300 counting rods on the desk. Moreover, if one imagines performing numerical-analysis computations with rods as long as 6 chon, the time spent arranging them in space would exceed the time spent finding the root or computing the rate of convergence.

Therefore, even judging by the size of a modern desk, it is not recommended to buy second-hand late-Joseon counting-rod sets (san-gaji) to do calculations; and if one were to buy several containers of them, the price would be far too high. In modern times, one may do the calculations using toothpicks. If it is hard to distinguish the colors, one may attach a toothpick diagonally to the negative numbers, or paint them with a brush beforehand.

If we suppose that number is an abstract concept arising in the course of measuring length, then higher-degree terms up to the cube, and equations as well, can be organized into easily understandable concepts.

Let us actually solve a problem. Given $f(x)=3x^3+2x+1$, the operation of computing $f(30.28)-f(30)$ is solved as follows.

Let x be the unit length of one ja (尺). The problem then becomes:

There is water amounting to 3 cubic ja, and there is a ruler 2 ja long. A stationery-store owner (1 person) wants to sell the two. The market merchants' association has decided that from now on, 1 ja will be standardized from 30.28 cm to 30 cm.
In that case, how much less water must be sold, and how much must the ruler be cut?

In this way, the constant term is externalized as the stationery-store owner and drops out of the calculation. Naturally, the object of computation becomes $f(30.28)-f(30)$ for $f(x)=3x^3+2x$; removing the constant term 1 shifts the entire graph downward by 1.

At this point, since ${f(30)}\lt{f(30.28)}$, one may interpret the change we seek, $f(30.28)-f(30)$, as having its origin in an everyday need.

The unification of the length of one ja at this time is the refining of the pitch pipes (yullyeo, 律呂); the change in the ruler's length is the length changing accordingly; and the change in volume transmitting a different value to the balance likewise follows from length. This is why the pitch pipes are said to be the guiding thread of the system of units.

If you connect the trajectory traced by the end of a balance beam when it is rotated one full turn about its center, you get 2πr. And if you treat the entire interior, filled in completely, as a plane, you get πr². Only by regarding the interior as a single plane is the concept of the circle completed.

As Master Shao (Shao Kangjie) said, the claim that computation began from the square, the circle, the flat, and the straight is a persuasive interpretation, considering that Western mathematics likewise begins with Euclidean geometry and extends into non-Euclidean geometry. Therefore, that the foundation of numbers lies in knowing the horizontal and the vertical so as to know high and low coordinates, and in knowing the angular and the round so as to measure them, is in effect a principle that explains a considerable part of Western geometry — from Euclid's Elements, through the Cartesian coordinate system and calculus, all the way to non-Euclidean geometry.
